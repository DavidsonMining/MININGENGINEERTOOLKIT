1. What is Mine Planning?

Mine planning is the process of determining how a mineral deposit will be developed, mined and managed throughout the life of a mining project.

It involves integrating geology, mining methods, mine design, equipment, production scheduling, economics, environmental management, safety and rehabilitation into a practical mining strategy.

A mine plan should therefore answer important questions such as:

  • What material will be mined?
  • Where is the ore located?
  • How will the ore and waste be extracted?
  • What equipment will be required?
  • How much material will be produced?
  • When will different areas of the deposit be mined?
  • What will the operation cost?
  • How will environmental and safety risks be managed?
  • How will the mine be rehabilitated and closed?

2. The Mine Planning Life Cycle

🔎 Exploration & Resource Definition

Mine planning begins with an understanding of the mineral deposit. Geological exploration provides information about the location, geometry, grade, continuity and geological characteristics of the mineralisation.

Drilling, sampling, geological modelling and resource estimation are important inputs into later mine-design decisions.

📊 Conceptual Study

Conceptual planning considers possible mining methods, production rates, processing options, infrastructure, capital requirements and operating conditions.

The objective is to determine whether a technically and economically reasonable mining concept can be developed.

📘 Pre-Feasibility Study

A pre-feasibility study develops the preferred project concept in greater detail. It may examine alternative mining methods, mine layouts, production schedules, equipment requirements, processing capacity and infrastructure.

📗 Feasibility Study

The feasibility stage develops the selected mine concept to a level suitable for detailed evaluation and project decision-making.

It normally integrates detailed mine design, scheduling, capital and operating costs, processing assumptions, environmental considerations, infrastructure and risk.

3. Geological Information

🪨 Geological Model

The geological model represents the interpreted three-dimensional distribution of rock types, mineralisation, structures and other geological characteristics.

Mine planners use geological information to determine where mining can take place and how the deposit may be divided into mineable areas.

📊 Grade and Orebody Characteristics

Important characteristics include ore grade, thickness, depth, dip, strike, continuity, density, rock strength and geological structures.

These characteristics influence the choice of mining method, equipment, production sequence and economic performance.

4. Mineral Resources and Ore Reserves

💎 Mineral Resources

Mineral resources represent concentrations of material with reasonable prospects for eventual economic extraction, subject to the applicable reporting framework.

⛏️ Ore Reserves

Ore reserves represent the economically mineable part of a measured and/or indicated mineral resource, demonstrated by appropriate studies and considering relevant modifying factors.

In practice, mine planning connects the geological resource model with mine design, economic assumptions and practical mining constraints.

5. Mining Method Selection

Selecting an appropriate mining method is one of the major decisions in mine planning.

Factors may include:

  • Depth of the orebody
  • Orebody shape and thickness
  • Dip and geological continuity
  • Rock mass characteristics
  • Ore grade and distribution
  • Required production rate
  • Geotechnical conditions
  • Water conditions
  • Surface constraints
  • Economic conditions
  • Safety requirements
  • Environmental considerations

6. Open-Pit Mine Design

🏔️ Pit Limits

Open-pit planning involves determining the practical and economic limits of the excavation.

The final pit configuration can be influenced by orebody geometry, economic assumptions, slope requirements, mining recovery, dilution and other operational constraints.

📏 Bench Design

Benches divide an open pit into working levels. Important design parameters include bench height, bench face angle, berm width and overall slope geometry.

🛣️ Haul Roads and Ramps

Haul roads and ramps provide access between different levels of the mine. Their design must consider factors such as width, gradient, traffic, drainage, turning requirements and operating safety.

7. Underground Mine Planning

🕳️ Underground Access

Underground mines may use shafts, declines or adits depending on the deposit and mining conditions. Access design must provide safe and efficient movement of people, materials, ore and equipment.

⛏️ Development

Development creates the underground openings required to reach and extract the orebody. It may include declines, crosscuts, levels, drives, raises and other mine workings.

🏗️ Stoping

Stoping is the process of extracting ore from an underground production area. The selected stope method depends on orebody geometry, rock conditions, production requirements and other constraints.

8. Mine Production Scheduling

Mine scheduling determines the sequence and timing of mining activities.

A schedule may be developed at different levels:

  • Life-of-mine schedule
  • Long-term schedule
  • Medium-term schedule
  • Short-term schedule
  • Daily or shift planning

Scheduling attempts to coordinate ore production, waste movement, equipment availability, processing requirements and other operational constraints.

9. Stripping Ratio

⛏️ Waste-to-Ore Relationship

In surface mining, the stripping ratio describes the quantity of waste or overburden that must be removed relative to the quantity of ore extracted.

The stripping ratio is an important consideration in open-pit mine planning because increasing waste movement can significantly affect mining costs and equipment requirements.

10. Equipment Selection

Mine planners select equipment based on production requirements and operating conditions.

Factors may include:

  • Required production rate
  • Material properties
  • Bucket or truck capacity
  • Haul distance
  • Cycle time
  • Operating hours
  • Equipment availability
  • Fuel consumption
  • Maintenance requirements
  • Capital and operating costs

11. Mine Infrastructure

Mine planning must account for the infrastructure required to support mining and processing operations.

  • Access roads
  • Workshops
  • Fuel facilities
  • Power supply
  • Water supply
  • Processing facilities
  • Ore stockpiles
  • Waste rock facilities
  • Tailings storage facilities
  • Offices and supporting facilities

12. Economics of Mine Planning

💰 Capital Cost

Capital expenditure includes major investments needed to establish or expand the mining operation, such as mine development, equipment, infrastructure and processing facilities.

💵 Operating Cost

Operating costs are the costs associated with running the mine. They may include labour, fuel, maintenance, explosives, power, consumables, processing and material movement.

📈 Economic Evaluation

Economic evaluation can consider production, revenue, operating costs, capital expenditure, taxation, royalties, financing assumptions and other relevant factors.

Common financial measures used in mining project evaluation include Net Present Value (NPV), Internal Rate of Return (IRR) and payback period.

13. Risk and Uncertainty

Mine plans are developed using information that may contain geological, technical, economic and operational uncertainty.

Important sources of uncertainty can include changes in commodity prices, geological interpretation, geotechnical conditions, equipment performance, operating costs and regulatory requirements.

14. Environmental and Social Considerations

Modern mine planning considers environmental and social issues throughout the mine life rather than treating them as separate activities.

  • Water management
  • Waste management
  • Dust and air quality
  • Noise and vibration
  • Land disturbance
  • Biodiversity
  • Community relationships
  • Heritage and cultural considerations
  • Rehabilitation

15. Progressive Rehabilitation

🌱 Rehabilitation During Mining

Rehabilitation should be considered during mine planning and, where practicable, undertaken progressively during operations.

Australian guidance emphasizes early consideration of rehabilitation opportunities, final landforms and post-mining land uses because mine layout and scheduling can affect the ability and cost of future rehabilitation.

Progressive rehabilitation can include activities such as reshaping disturbed land, managing topsoil, revegetation and stabilising completed areas.

16. Mine Closure Planning

🔒 Closure is Part of Mine Planning

Mine closure should be considered from the early stages of project development and progressively refined as the mine develops.

Closure planning may address infrastructure removal, final landforms, water management, rehabilitation, monitoring and post-mining land use.

Australian guidance describes mine closure as part of the mine life cycle, with planning becoming more detailed as the operation approaches closure.

17. Typical Mine Life Cycle

🔎 Exploration

Identify and investigate potential mineral deposits.

📊 Resource Definition

Collect geological information and develop a resource model.

📘 Pre-Feasibility

Compare project concepts and develop preliminary designs and economic evaluations.

📗 Feasibility

Develop detailed technical, economic, environmental and operational plans.

🏗️ Development

Construct infrastructure and develop the mine.

⛏️ Operations

Extract, process and transport mineral products.

🌱 Progressive Rehabilitation

Rehabilitate suitable disturbed areas during the operating life.

🔒 Closure

Decommission, rehabilitate and prepare the site for its approved or agreed post-mining land use.

🌍 Post-Closure

Monitor the site and maintain rehabilitation and environmental controls where required.

18. Key Mine Planning Terms

Life of Mine (LOM)

The expected period over which mining operations are planned to continue.

Cut-off Grade

A grade criterion used to distinguish material that may be economically processed from material that may not meet the relevant economic conditions.

Dilution

The inclusion of waste or lower-grade material with the ore during mining.

Mining Recovery

The proportion of the economically mineable material that is successfully extracted.

Stockpile

A designated area where mined material is temporarily stored before further handling or processing.

Final Pit

The planned ultimate excavation boundary of an open-pit operation under the selected assumptions and constraints.

19. Important Principle

A good mine plan is not simply a drawing of where mining will occur.

It is an integrated plan that connects the mineral resource, mining method, mine design, production schedule, equipment, economics, environmental management, safety, rehabilitation and closure.

The plan should also be reviewed and updated as new geological, operational, economic and environmental information becomes available.
