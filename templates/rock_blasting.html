🧨 Rock Blasting

A practical overview of rock blasting, blast design, explosives, fragmentation, safety and performance.

1. What is Rock Blasting?

Rock blasting is the controlled use of explosives to break rock into smaller and more manageable fragments for excavation, loading and transportation.

In mining, blasting is commonly used in both surface and underground operations where the rock is too competent to be excavated efficiently by mechanical methods alone.

A successful blast should produce suitable fragmentation while controlling unwanted effects such as excessive ground vibration, airblast, flyrock and damage to surrounding rock.

2. Main Objectives of Blasting

  • Break the rock into suitable fragments.
  • Produce the required fragmentation size.
  • Make loading and hauling more efficient.
  • Reduce excessive oversize material.
  • Control unwanted ground vibration.
  • Control flyrock and airblast.
  • Maintain safe working conditions.
  • Minimise unnecessary damage to surrounding rock.

3. Basic Blasting Cycle

📍 Planning

The blast area is studied and the required excavation and fragmentation objectives are defined.

📐 Blast Layout

The blast pattern and relevant design parameters are established according to the mine's approved blasting practices and engineering requirements.

🕳️ Drilling

Blast holes are drilled according to the planned pattern and required depth.

🧨 Charging

Explosives and approved initiation systems are placed according to the blast plan and applicable safety procedures.

🚧 Stemming

Suitable stemming material is placed where required to help confine the explosive energy within the blast hole.

⚡ Initiation

The approved initiation system is used to initiate the blast under controlled conditions.

🔎 Post-Blast Inspection

The blasted area is inspected for fragmentation, misfires, unsafe conditions and other blast results before normal operations resume.

4. Important Blasting Terms

Burden

The approximate distance between a blast hole and the nearest free face or between rows of blast holes, depending on the blast configuration.

Spacing

The distance between adjacent blast holes in the same row.

Bench Height

The vertical height of the bench being blasted.

Hole Depth

The total drilled depth of a blast hole.

Stemming

Material placed in part of a blast hole to help confine explosive gases and energy.

Subdrilling

Additional drilling below the planned floor level that may be used to help achieve the required excavation floor.

Fragmentation

The size distribution of rock produced after blasting.

5. Blast Pattern

A blast pattern describes the arrangement of blast holes across the area to be blasted.

Two important pattern dimensions are commonly described as burden and spacing.

The selected pattern depends on factors such as rock properties, bench geometry, drilling equipment, required fragmentation and the approved blast design.

6. Blast Volume

A simplified blast-volume estimate can be obtained from the blast pattern and bench dimensions.

In the Mining Engineer Toolkit, the Blasting Calculator can be used to estimate blast volume from burden, spacing, bench height and number of holes.

7. Powder Factor

Powder factor is a measure relating the quantity of explosive used to the amount of rock blasted.

It is commonly expressed as explosive mass per volume of rock or explosive mass per mass of rock, depending on the convention being used.

Powder factor is useful when evaluating blast performance and comparing blasting conditions.

8. Explosives in Mining

🧨 Explosive

An explosive is a material capable of releasing energy rapidly through a chemical reaction.

💥 Energy Release

During blasting, the released energy produces pressure and stress waves that interact with the surrounding rock.

🪨 Rock Breakage

The interaction between explosive energy and the rock mass creates fractures and breaks the rock into fragments.

9. Rock Properties Affecting Blasting

  • Rock strength
  • Rock density
  • Elastic properties
  • Jointing and fractures
  • Faults and geological structures
  • Weathering
  • Water conditions
  • Rock mass discontinuities

Geological conditions can strongly influence fragmentation and the distribution of blast energy.

10. Fragmentation

Fragmentation describes the distribution of rock sizes produced by a blast.

Good fragmentation can improve downstream loading, hauling and crushing operations.

Excessive oversize may require secondary breaking, while excessive fines may also create handling and processing challenges.

11. Blast Performance

Blast performance can be evaluated using several observations and measurements.

  • Fragmentation
  • Diggability
  • Muckpile shape
  • Oversize generation
  • Backbreak
  • Ground vibration
  • Airblast
  • Flyrock control
  • Production results

12. Ground Vibration

Ground vibration is the movement of the ground generated by blasting.

Vibration monitoring can be used to assess blast effects and help manage potential impacts on nearby infrastructure and sensitive areas.

13. Flyrock and Airblast

🪨 Flyrock

Flyrock refers to rock fragments that are projected beyond the intended blast area.

🌬️ Airblast

Airblast is a pressure wave generated by blasting that travels through the atmosphere.

Both require appropriate controls, exclusion zones, monitoring and strict adherence to approved blasting procedures.

14. Misfires

A misfire occurs when an explosive charge or part of a blast does not initiate as intended.

Misfires are serious safety hazards. They must be managed only by trained and authorised personnel using the mine's approved procedures and applicable regulations.

15. Blasting Safety

Blasting requires strict safety management because explosives can cause serious injury, fatalities and property damage when handled incorrectly.

  • Only authorised personnel should handle explosives.
  • Approved blast plans and procedures must be followed.
  • Blast areas must be properly controlled.
  • Personnel must be cleared from the exclusion zone.
  • Warning and communication systems must be used.
  • Post-blast inspections must be completed.
  • Misfires must be handled according to approved procedures.

16. Environmental Considerations

  • Ground vibration
  • Airblast
  • Dust
  • Noise
  • Flyrock
  • Water quality
  • Effects on nearby infrastructure

Environmental controls should be considered during blast planning and reviewed using monitoring results and applicable requirements.

17. Blasting and the Mining Cycle

Blasting is closely connected to the rest of the mining production cycle.

Drilling → Blasting → Loading → Hauling → Crushing/Processing

The quality of fragmentation produced by blasting can influence the efficiency of subsequent loading, hauling and crushing activities.

18. Key Principle

Effective blasting is not simply about using explosives to break rock.

It involves integrating geology, rock mass characteristics, drilling, blast planning, fragmentation, safety, environmental controls and downstream production requirements.
